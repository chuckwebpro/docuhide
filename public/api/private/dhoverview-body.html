One document. A different, encrypted copy for every reader.

DocuHide takes a single parent document and generates a child for each recipient. Each child holds only what that recipient is cleared to see, the removed content leaves no trace, and each copy is encrypted for its own reader. Access can be cut off for one person without touching anyone else.

Cut one parent into children

Pick a recipient and a method. Black-bar redaction keeps the page shape and marks every cut. Tokenization, the approach IBM describes, swaps each cut for a replacement token that maps back to the original. A DocuHide child closes the gaps and substitutes nothing, so there is no bar, token or mapping to follow. The memo is an invented example.

DocuSign

Containment, not convenience

DocuSign proves who signed what and when. DocuHide controls what each reader can see inside the document, before and after signing. DocuSign controls which documents a recipient sees in an envelope. DocuHide controls what a recipient sees within a document.

Where DocuHide leads
  • Content-level control. Each reader gets a different, encrypted version of the same document, and cut content leaves no trace.
  • After signature. Revocation, time limits and print and copy rules apply per recipient to the child they hold.
  • Leak attribution. A leaked copy matches one recipient's version. Attorney confirmation still needed.
Where DocuSign leads today
  • Installed base and workflow. It is the agreement standard, with integrations DocuHide does not have.
  • Audited trust. Independent attestations and government authorizations that take years to earn.
  • A shipping product. DocuHide's redaction engine is not built yet.

What this section does not claim. It does not say DocuHide replaces DocuSign. The evidence supports a layer that controls content around a signing workflow. Compliance for DocuHide is a design goal, not a certification. Market size, buyout and funding claims belong in an investor document, not in a technical comparison.

Who benefits

Every field that shares one document with many readers

The pattern is the same everywhere: one source document, readers with different clearances, and a duty to keep the rest private. DocuHide supports these regimes. It does not certify compliance, which depends on how an organization deploys it and what its auditors accept.

Post-quantum

Designed for the quantum threat since 1999

Peter Shor published his factoring algorithm in 1994. It is the one quantum algorithm that breaks the public-key cryptography the internet runs on, including 256-bit elliptic curves. Lov Grover followed in 1996 with a search speedup that weakens symmetric keys but does not break them. Warshaw diagrammed DocuHide in December 1999 with both in view, and holds the patent.

1994

Shor

Efficient factoring and discrete logarithms on a quantum computer. Breaks RSA and elliptic-curve keys of any practical size.

1996

Grover

Quadratic speedup for brute-force search. A 256-bit symmetric key keeps roughly 128 bits of strength, which is still considered safe.

What a quantum attacker gets

Less to steal, and no stand-in to reverse

  • Removed content is absent. A child never contains the cut text, so there is nothing for a future machine to decrypt.
  • No token or mask. Tokenization leaves a replacement that maps back to the original. DocuHide leaves nothing, so there is no mapping to reverse and no trail for a person or an AI tool to unmask.
  • One key per child. Breaking one recipient's copy exposes only that recipient's view.
Where the rivals stand

Post-quantum is on their roadmaps

  • Microsoft: rollout from 2029, full transition by 2033 (single secondary source).
  • Adobe: ML-DSA signing tested May 2025, PDF specification pending.
  • DocuSign: ML-DSA support started, migration dates due in 2026; downloaded PDFs use RSA-4096 today.
  • IBM: Kyber and Dilithium on IBM Z, at the infrastructure layer.
  • Oracle: ML-KEM and ML-DSA in Java since JDK 24 (March 2025); a PQC product is announced, not yet reviewed.
